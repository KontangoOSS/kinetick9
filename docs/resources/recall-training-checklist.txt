INTRODUCTION
------------
A reliable recall — the ability to call your dog back to you
under any circumstances — is quite possibly the most important
behavior you will ever teach. It is also a genuine life-saving
skill. A solid recall can prevent your dog from running into
traffic, approaching a dangerous animal, or getting lost.

The good news: with the right approach, any dog at any age
can develop a rock-solid recall. This checklist will guide
you through building that behavior step by step.

THE GOLDEN RULE OF RECALL:
"Come" should ALWAYS predict something wonderful.
Every. Single. Time.
Never call your dog to you for something unpleasant
(nail trims, leaving the dog park, a bath). Go to them
for those things instead. Protect your recall word fiercely.


================================================================
PHASE 1: FOUNDATION — BUILDING THE CONDITIONED RESPONSE
================================================================

□ STEP 1 — CHOOSE YOUR RECALL CUE
  Pick ONE word and stick with it consistently.
  Common choices: "Come," "Here," "Front"
  Consider using a unique word your dog has never heard
  before, so it carries no history of being ignored.
  Some trainers use a whistle — consistent and carries
  over long distances.

□ STEP 2 — CHARGE YOUR RECALL WORD (INDOORS)
  Say your recall cue ONCE in a happy, upbeat tone.
  The moment your dog orients toward you or takes a
  step in your direction, mark with "yes!" and deliver
  3–5 high-value treats in rapid succession.
  Practice 10–15 repetitions per session.
  Do this in a low-distraction environment only.

□ STEP 3 — USE ONLY YOUR BEST REWARDS
  Recall must be the MOST rewarding behavior in your
  dog's repertoire. Use:
  - Real meat (chicken, beef, hot dogs)
  - Cheese
  - Their absolute favorite toy + play session
  - Or whatever your dog goes absolutely crazy for
  Dry kibble is NOT sufficient for recall training.

□ STEP 4 — MAKE ARRIVAL THE BEST PART
  When your dog reaches you, make it a PARTY.
  - Multiple treats delivered one by one
  - Enthusiastic verbal praise
  - A brief play session with their favorite toy
  The moment of arrival should feel like winning the
  jackpot every single time.

□ STEP 5 — NEVER REPEAT THE CUE
  Say "come" ONCE. If your dog does not respond, do not
  repeat it. Instead, make yourself more interesting:
  - Run away from your dog (prey drive kicks in)
  - Crouch down and open your arms
  - Make a funny sound
  If your dog still doesn't come, go get them calmly.
  Never chase — this teaches them that running away from
  you is a fun game.


================================================================
PHASE 2: BUILDING DISTANCE & DURATION
================================================================

□ STEP 6 — PRACTICE "RESTRAINED RECALLS"
  Have a helper gently hold your dog while you back away,
  building excitement. Call your dog and have your helper
  release them. The restrained recall builds drive and
  enthusiasm toward you.

□ STEP 7 — USE A LONG LINE (15–30 FEET)
  Before practicing off-leash, use a long training lead.
  This gives your dog freedom to explore while ensuring
  you can guide them back if needed. Never let the line
  go taut — use it only as a backup safety measure.

□ STEP 8 — RECALL FROM DIFFERENT ROOMS
  Practice inside your home. Call from another room.
  Reward heavily when your dog finds you.
  This builds the habit of orienting to you when called,
  even when they cannot see you.

□ STEP 9 — GRADUALLY INCREASE DISTANCE
  Add distance in small increments:
  - 5 feet → 10 feet → 20 feet → 30 feet
  Never push distance faster than your dog's success rate.
  If they fail twice in a row, reduce the distance.

□ STEP 10 — PROOF DURATION AT EACH DISTANCE
  Before increasing distance, practice 10 successful
  recalls at your current distance across 3 separate
  sessions. Consistency at one level before moving on.


================================================================
PHASE 3: ADDING DISTRACTIONS (THE REAL WORLD)
================================================================

□ STEP 11 — DISTRACTION HIERARCHY
  Introduce distractions gradually, from easiest to hardest:
  LOW:     Practicing in a new room indoors
  MEDIUM:  Backyard with mild environmental stimulation
  HIGH:    Quiet park with few people
  VERY HIGH: Park with other dogs, people, squirrels

  Always begin new distraction levels at SHORT distances
  and build back up.

□ STEP 12 — PRACTICE "FIND IT" THEN RECALL
  Toss a treat on the ground (dog sniffs for it).
  While they are sniffing — call "come!"
  This introduces the crucial skill of disengaging from
  an interesting distraction to return to you.
  Reward massively when they succeed.

□ STEP 13 — THE "AROUND OTHER DOGS" PROTOCOL
  This is often the hardest distraction.
  Step 1: Practice recall when another dog is visible
          but at a distance (50+ feet). Reward big.
  Step 2: Reduce distance to 30 feet over multiple sessions.
  Step 3: Practice when another dog is nearby but calm.
  Step 4: Proof with multiple dogs present.
  Never start this step in a dog park setting.

□ STEP 14 — PRACTICE RANDOM REAL-LIFE RECALLS
  Throughout your daily life, randomly call your dog,
  reward generously, then release them back to what
  they were doing ("go play" or "free").
  This teaches them that "come" doesn't always mean
  the fun ends — sometimes they get a reward and go
  right back to their activity.

□ STEP 15 — THE "TOUCH" (TARGET) RECALL
  Teach your dog to touch your open palm with their nose.
  Hold out your hand, say "touch." When they nose your
  palm, mark and reward. Once solid, use "touch" as an
  alternative recall for dogs who stop just out of reach.
  "Touch" brings them all the way into hand contact.


================================================================
PHASE 4: ADVANCED PROOFING
================================================================

□ STEP 16 — OFF-LEASH IN A FENCED AREA
  Before going fully off-leash in open spaces, practice
  extensively in a safely fenced area with realistic
  distractions. A tennis court, baseball diamond, or
  rented training facility works well.

□ STEP 17 — VARYING REWARD TYPES
  To prevent your dog from only coming when they see a
  treat, vary your rewards unpredictably:
  - Sometimes treats
  - Sometimes a toy and game of tug
  - Sometimes an exuberant play session
  - Sometimes just real, genuine praise (once behavior
    is very well established)

□ STEP 18 — THE 3-D'S PROOFING TEST
  Before declaring your recall reliable, test it against:
  DISTANCE:    20+ feet away
  DURATION:    Dog is engaged in something interesting
  DISTRACTION: At least 3 high-value distractions present
  If your dog passes 9 out of 10 trials, congratulations!
  You have a reliable recall.

□ STEP 19 — EMERGENCY RECALL
  Separately from your everyday recall, teach an emergency
  recall using a different word or a whistle. Charge this
  cue with the highest value rewards possible (real meat).
  Reserve it ONLY for genuine emergencies. Never call with
  this word unless you have an outstanding reward ready.

□ STEP 20 — MAINTENANCE PRACTICE
  A recall is not "trained and done." Practice it regularly
  throughout your dog's life — at least several times per
  week. Dogs need regular reinforcement to keep behaviors
  strong. Occasional jackpot rewards keep recall enthusiasm
  high for years to come.


================================================================
TROUBLESHOOTING COMMON RECALL PROBLEMS
================================================================

PROBLEM: My dog comes but stops just out of reach.
SOLUTION: Teach the "touch" nose target (Step 15). Also,
check that you haven't inadvertently grabbed your dog's
collar as punishment when they arrive. Dogs quickly learn
to hover just out of reach to avoid unpleasant handling.
Practice "collar grabs" — grab the collar, reward, release.

PROBLEM: My dog comes fast then runs past me.
SOLUTION: Crouch down as they approach. Turn slightly
sideways (less threatening body language). Reward before
they have a chance to blow past.

PROBLEM: My dog completely ignores me at the dog park.
SOLUTION: This means your recall isn't strong enough yet
to handle that level of distraction. Go back to Phase 3
and build up systematically. Never call your dog at a
distraction level beyond where they currently succeed.

PROBLEM: My dog used to have a great recall but now ignores.
SOLUTION: Your reward history has likely depleted. Go back
to Phase 1 and re-charge the cue with high-value rewards
for several weeks. Also check: Is "come" predicting
something unpleasant? (End of fun, nail trims, etc.)

PROBLEM: My dog comes reliably at home but not outside.
SOLUTION: Behaviors do NOT automatically generalize to new
environments. "Come" in your living room and "come" in the
park are two different behaviors to your dog. Begin Phase 3
from the beginning in each new environment.

QUICK REFERENCE — THE RECALL COMMANDMENTS
================================================================

  ✦  "Come" always predicts something wonderful
  ✦  Say the cue ONCE — never repeat it
  ✦  Use your highest value rewards for recall only
  ✦  Make arrival a celebration every single time
  ✦  Never call your dog for something they dislike
  ✦  Build distractions gradually and systematically
  ✦  Practice randomly throughout daily life
  ✦  Maintain with regular rewards throughout your
     dog's entire life
  ✦  If in doubt, go back a step — never rush it
  ✦  A long line is your safety net; use it until
     you have earned off-leash trust
