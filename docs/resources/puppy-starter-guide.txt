INTRODUCTION
------------
Congratulations on your new puppy! The first 30 days in your
home are the most critical period in your dog's development.
The experiences, routines, and boundaries you establish now
will shape your puppy's behavior, confidence, and personality
for life. This guide will walk you through everything you need
to know to set your puppy — and yourself — up for success.


SECTION 1: THE FIRST 48 HOURS — SETTLING IN
--------------------------------------------
The first two days are about helping your puppy feel safe and
secure in their new environment. Resist the urge to invite
everyone over to meet the new puppy. Instead, keep things calm
and predictable.

* Set up a designated "puppy zone" — a playpen or gated area
  where your puppy can be safely confined when unsupervised.

* Place their crate in this zone with the door open. Put a
  soft blanket and a worn t-shirt of yours inside so they
  can get used to your scent.

* Introduce your puppy to one room at a time. Giving access
  to the whole house too quickly is overwhelming and makes
  house training much harder.

* Keep greetings calm. Loud voices and chaotic energy will
  increase your puppy's arousal levels, making it harder for
  them to settle.

* Expect some whining the first couple of nights. This is
  completely normal — your puppy has just left their mother
  and littermates. A ticking clock wrapped in a towel near
  the crate can help simulate a heartbeat.


SECTION 2: CRATE TRAINING BASICS
---------------------------------
A crate is one of the most valuable tools you have. When used
correctly, most dogs come to love their crate as a safe haven.

WHY CRATE TRAIN?
- Prevents destructive behavior when unsupervised
- Dramatically speeds up house training
- Gives your puppy a safe, quiet space of their own
- Reduces anxiety in new environments

HOW TO INTRODUCE THE CRATE:
Step 1 — Toss high-value treats near and inside the crate.
          Let your puppy explore at their own pace. Never
          force them in.

Step 2 — Feed meals inside the crate with the door open.
          Once comfortable, close the door during meals and
          open it when they finish.

Step 3 — Begin closing the door for short periods (5–10
          minutes) while you are in the room. Gradually
          increase duration.

Step 4 — Practice leaving the room while puppy is in the
          crate. Return before they fuss.

Step 5 — Begin leaving the house for short periods. Always
          exercise your puppy before crating.

CRATE TIME GUIDELINES BY AGE:
- 8–10 weeks:   1 hour maximum at a time (daytime)
- 10–12 weeks:  2 hours maximum at a time (daytime)
- 3–4 months:   3 hours maximum at a time
- 4–6 months:   4 hours maximum at a time
- 6+ months:    No more than 5–6 hours at a time

NOTE: These guidelines are for daytime. At night, most
puppies can hold longer once they are sleeping soundly.


SECTION 3: HOUSE TRAINING
--------------------------
House training success depends on three things: supervision,
consistency, and a solid schedule. Most puppies can be
reliably house trained within 4–8 weeks if you follow these
guidelines closely.

THE GOLDEN RULE: If you can't supervise, confine.

BASIC SCHEDULE:
Your puppy needs to go outside:
  - Immediately upon waking (every morning and after naps)
  - Within 15 minutes of eating or drinking
  - After play sessions
  - Before and after being crated
  - Every 1–2 hours during the day (young puppies)

WHEN YOU TAKE THEM OUTSIDE:
  - Go to the same spot each time. The familiar scent will
    trigger them to go faster.
  - Use a cue word like "go potty" or "get busy" while they
    are eliminating.
  - The moment they finish, immediately reward with a treat
    and calm praise. Timing is everything — reward WHILE
    they are going, not after you return inside.
  - Don't rush back inside. Many puppies hold it, then go
    as soon as you return — give them a full 5 minutes.

ACCIDENTS:
  - Never scold or punish after the fact. Puppies cannot
    connect a punishment to something that happened more
    than 2 seconds ago.
  - Simply clean it up with an enzymatic cleaner (like
    Nature's Miracle) to eliminate the odor.
  - Ask yourself: What missed me? Did I miss a signal?
    Was it too long between bathroom breaks?


SECTION 4: THE FIRST COMMANDS TO TEACH
---------------------------------------
Start training from day one — puppies as young as 8 weeks
are ready to learn! Keep sessions short (2–5 minutes),
fun, and always end on a positive note.

1. NAME RECOGNITION
   Say your puppy's name once. The moment they look at you,
   mark with "yes!" and reward with a treat. Practice 10
   times per session, several sessions per day. Within a
   week, your puppy will be reliably orienting to their name.

2. SIT
   Hold a treat close to your puppy's nose and slowly move
   your hand up. Their bottom will naturally lower as their
   nose follows the treat. The moment their bottom touches
   the floor, say "yes!" and give the treat. Once the
   behavior is consistent, add the verbal cue "sit" just
   before you lure.

3. DOWN
   From a sit, hold a treat at your puppy's nose and slowly
   bring it straight down to the floor between their paws.
   As they follow, their elbows will drop. Mark and reward
   the moment their belly touches the floor.

4. COME (RECALL)
   Kneel down, open your arms, and cheerfully call your
   puppy's name followed by "come!" When they reach you,
   reward heavily with treats and happy praise. NEVER call
   your puppy to you for something they dislike (like a
   bath or nail trim) — always go to them for those things.

5. LEAVE IT
   Place a treat on the floor. Cover it with your hand.
   Wait for your puppy to stop pawing, sniffing, and
   licking your hand. The moment they pull back or look at
   you, say "yes!" and reward from your OTHER hand. This
   is the start of a powerful impulse control behavior.


SECTION 5: SOCIALIZATION — THE MOST IMPORTANT JOB
---------------------------------------------------
The socialization window is the most critical and time-
limited developmental period in your puppy's life. It closes
around 12–16 weeks of age. During this window, positive
exposures to a wide variety of people, animals, sounds,
surfaces, and environments will shape your dog's confidence
and temperament for the rest of their life.

THE GOAL: Create positive, low-stress associations with as
many new things as possible before the window closes.

SOCIALIZATION CHECKLIST — Aim to expose your puppy to:

PEOPLE:
  □ Men with hats, beards, glasses
  □ Women of various appearances
  □ Children of different ages
  □ People in uniforms (mail carrier, vet scrubs)
  □ People using walkers, wheelchairs, crutches
  □ People with umbrellas or carrying large objects

ENVIRONMENTS:
  □ Veterinary office (happy visits — no procedures!)
  □ Pet supply store
  □ Parking lots and busy sidewalks
  □ Elevators and staircases
  □ Grooming salon (just a visit!)
  □ Different flooring: tile, hardwood, grass, gravel, metal

SOUNDS:
  □ Thunderstorm sounds (use YouTube, start at low volume)
  □ Traffic and city sounds
  □ Vacuum cleaner and appliances
  □ Children playing
  □ Fireworks (again, start low volume)

ANIMALS:
  □ Calm, vaccinated, dog-friendly dogs
  □ Cats (if applicable)

IMPORTANT NOTE: Until your puppy is fully vaccinated, avoid
areas with unknown dogs or high dog traffic (dog parks,
pet stores with unknown animals). Carry your puppy when
in doubt and prioritize socialization in controlled settings.


SECTION 6: BITE INHIBITION
---------------------------
Puppies explore the world with their mouths, and all puppies
bite. Teaching bite inhibition — how to control the force of
their mouth — is one of the most important lessons of
puppyhood.

STEP 1 — TEACHING SOFT MOUTH:
When puppy bites hard during play, let out a sharp "ouch!"
and briefly stop all interaction (turn away, freeze). This
mimics how their littermates communicated that biting was
too hard. Resume play after a few seconds.

STEP 2 — REDIRECTING:
Always have chew toys available. When your puppy begins
to mouth, redirect to an appropriate toy. Reward any
interaction with the toy with praise.

STEP 3 — MANAGEMENT:
Puppies bite more when they are tired, over-stimulated, or
over-aroused. Learn to recognize your puppy's "witching hour"
and proactively give them a nap in their crate before the
biting escalates.

WHAT NOT TO DO:
- Do NOT hold your puppy's mouth closed
- Do NOT tap or flick your puppy's nose
- Do NOT scruff your puppy as punishment
These methods teach your puppy that human hands are
unpredictable and scary, undermining trust.


SECTION 7: DAILY ROUTINE SAMPLE
---------------------------------
Structure and predictability reduce anxiety in puppies.
Below is a sample routine for an 8–12 week old puppy:

7:00 AM  — Wake up. Immediately outside for bathroom.
            Brief play session in the yard.
7:30 AM  — Breakfast (feed in crate or in Kong toy).
            Outside immediately after eating.
8:00 AM  — Active play & training session (5 minutes).
9:00 AM  — Nap in crate (1–2 hours).
11:00 AM — Outside for bathroom. Brief play session.
11:30 AM — Training session (5 minutes). Quiet chew time.
12:00 PM — Lunch. Outside immediately after.
1:00 PM  — Nap in crate (1–2 hours).
3:00 PM  — Outside for bathroom. Socialization outing.
5:00 PM  — Active play & training session.
5:30 PM  — Dinner. Outside immediately after.
7:00 PM  — Calm evening play and snuggle time.
9:00 PM  — Final bathroom trip outside.
9:30 PM  — Bedtime in crate.


SECTION 8: WHAT NOT TO WORRY ABOUT (YET)
------------------------------------------
New puppy owners often panic about behaviors that are
completely normal and developmentally appropriate:

* Zoomies (FRAPs — Frenetic Random Activity Periods):
  Normal energy release. Let it happen safely.

* Eating grass: Usually harmless. Monitor for vomiting.

* Chasing their tail: Normal play behavior in young pups.

* Not responding to commands in new environments:
  Completely normal! Generalization takes time and practice.

* Sleeping a LOT: Puppies need 16–20 hours of sleep per day.
  Enforcing nap time in the crate is healthy, not cruel.


SECTION 9: WHEN TO CALL A PROFESSIONAL
-----------------------------------------
Some behaviors require professional guidance early. Reach
out to us if you notice:

  - Growling or snapping at family members
  - Extreme fear responses that don't improve with exposure
  - Resource guarding of food, toys, or spaces
  - Persistent biting that isn't improving by 12 weeks
  - Signs of separation anxiety (excessive vocalization,
    destruction when left alone)

Early intervention always produces better outcomes.
Don't wait until a behavior becomes a habit.
